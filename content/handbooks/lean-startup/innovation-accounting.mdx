<InOneMinute>

- Normal financial reporting says nothing about a product with no customers yet, so a startup needs its own way to show progress.
- Three steps: get a baseline, tune the engine toward the target, then decide whether to pivot or keep going.
- Set learning milestones the way you would set any goal: specific, measurable, and dated.
- This is the boring principle, and it is the one that keeps a team honest when the numbers are flat.

</InOneMinute>

## The problem it solves

"We are making great progress" is unfalsifiable. Without revenue or a plan to compare against, a team can work hard for a year and nobody can say whether it went well.

Innovation accounting fixes the reporting gap. It gives you something real to hold a team to before there is a profit and loss statement worth reading.

## Three steps

| Step | What you do |
|---|---|
| **1. Establish the baseline** | Build an MVP and measure how the product performs today, however bad. That is your starting line |
| **2. Tune the engine** | Change one thing at a time and move the baseline toward the target. Every experiment should shift a number you named in advance |
| **3. Pivot or persevere** | When the numbers stop moving toward the target, change direction rather than keep tuning |

The order matters. Teams often skip step one because the early numbers are embarrassing. Without a baseline, later improvements cannot be shown.

## Learning milestones

Set goals about what you will know, not what you will ship. Treat them like any other goal: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and dated.

| Weak | Better |
|---|---|
| "Validate the onboarding idea" | "By 30 April, get activation from 42% to 55% for one cohort" |
| "Improve retention" | "By Q3, 30-day retention above 15% for two cohorts running" |
| "Ship the referral feature" | "By 15 May, know whether referrals bring one in ten new signups" |

The right-hand column can fail. That is the point of it.

## Cohort analysis

Cohorts are the main reporting tool here, because they separate "more people arrived" from "the product got better".

<CohortAnalysisDiagram />

Track each cohort through the same stages: people who visited, signed up, subscribed, and stayed active. Percentages, not totals. If the percentages are flat while the totals climb, the product has not improved and the marketing spend has.

<Callout type="warn" title="Flat cohorts after several experiments">
That is a signal, not a reason to try harder. A run of tuning experiments that does not move the numbers is the clearest evidence you have that the direction is wrong.
</Callout>

## After a release

Every feature is an experiment whether you treat it as one or not. When something ships, check:

- Are people using it at all?
- Did it change the number it was supposed to change?
- Did it bring new signups, or only please people who were already staying?

Write the expected answer down before the release. Comparing what you expected against what happened is most of the value.
