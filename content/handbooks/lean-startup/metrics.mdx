<InOneMinute>

- Vanity metrics rise no matter what you do. Total users, total pageviews, cumulative anything.
- A useful metric passes three tests, which Ries names Actionable, Accessible and Auditable.
- Split testing is the way to know a change caused the result rather than coincided with it.
- Report by cohort. A total hides whether the product is getting better.

</InOneMinute>

## Vanity metrics

Some numbers only go up. Total registered users never falls, so it always looks like progress, whatever the product is doing.

| Vanity | Better |
|---|---|
| Total registered users | Share of this month's signups still active at 30 days |
| Total pageviews | Views per visitor, by cohort |
| Cumulative downloads | Downloads that reach first use |
| Hits or raw visits | Completed signups |

The tell is simple. If a number cannot go down when you make the product worse, it cannot tell you when you made it better.

## The three tests, or the three A's

Ries names three things a real metric must be.

| Test | Means | In practice |
|---|---|---|
| **Actionable** | Shows clear cause and effect | You can name what you changed and what moved. Use views per visitor, not raw visits |
| **Accessible** | Everyone can see and read it | A weekly summary in plain language, not a dashboard only the analyst opens |
| **Auditable** | Someone owns it, and it can be checked | A named person can explain where a number came from, and the team can verify it by hand if it looks wrong |

Auditable gets skipped most. When a surprising number arrives and nobody can say where it came from, the discussion becomes an argument about the data instead of a decision about the product.

## Split testing

Run two versions at once and compare. This is the tool that turns opinions into answers.

- Test one change at a time, or you will not know which part did the work.
- Decide the success threshold before you start.
- Let it run long enough to mean something. Small samples produce confident nonsense in both directions.

<Callout type="note" title="Test the things nobody wants to test">
The features everyone assumes are essential are the best candidates. Removing one in a split test is cheap, and teams are routinely wrong about which parts customers care about.
</Callout>

## Report by cohort

A cohort is the group of people who arrived in the same period. Following each group separately shows whether the product is improving, which a total cannot.

<CohortAnalysisDiagram />

Track each cohort through the same stages the diagram shows: signed up, activated, subscribed, still active at 30 days. Percentages, not totals.

After every release, check what it did to the cohort that met it. Are people using the new thing? Did it change signups, activation, or retention? Write down what you expect before it ships. A feature that moves nothing is information too.

<CheckItWorked>
Take the number your team reports most often. Ask what would make it go down. If nothing realistic would, it is a vanity metric.
</CheckItWorked>
